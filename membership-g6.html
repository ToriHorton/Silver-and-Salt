Why one hundred

One woman with one checkbook cannot move a number like that. One hundred of us can.

Every part of this is designed to be carried by a lot of people doing a little, at the same time.

One hundred women learning

Six classes, four workshops, and a season of gatherings. Investing becomes something we know how to do, together, instead of something other people know how to do.

Forty hours on every company

Real diligence is slow and expensive for one person working alone. Split across a community, it happens on every company we finish.

Checks that add up to a round

Small checks pooled together arrive as one investment a founder can build on, and as a name that Utah founders learn to call.

This works when one hundred people show up and each do a little. That is the whole plan.

Following along from a distance is welcome, and it is how a lot of people start. Showing up is the part that changes the number.

Your first year, in order.

First

You meet Tori, and then you meet the women.

Every membership starts with a conversation. You get the season calendar, an introduction to the community, and a clear picture of what you just joined.

January

Your first class.

Six sessions with Invest For Better, the national program that teaches women to invest with their values. You learn the vocabulary, the math, and the questions to ask, with women who are learning it at the same time.

All season

Your first workshop, and the gatherings in between.

Wealth experts come to teach in person four times a year. Drop-in hours run monthly. This is where most of the membership actually happens.

Quarterly

Your first pitch meeting.

A Utah founder pitches live. You read the memo the community wrote after forty hours of research, and you ask her anything you want to know.

When you are ready

Your first yes.

Deals open one company at a time. Your money stays in your own account until you say yes, and accredited investors decide deal by deal. Saying no is a normal, common answer.

Every year

Your first receipt.

We publish what the community did with its money: the companies we met, the checks we wrote, the women we taught, and the gift we gave.

The mechanics, in detail

How a deal closes

When enough members say yes, the deal closes through an SPV (a special purpose vehicle): one shared account for that single company, which appears as one clean line on the founder's cap table. Easier for the founder, and it lets you spread smaller checks across more companies.

What happens if a company succeeds

Investors are repaid first. Profits then split 80/20, and the collective's 20 percent is shared again among the woman who led the research, the community, and the organization. The full split will be published in the receipt.

Who can invest

Federal rules decide who can invest in private companies, and they limit it to accredited investors: annual income above $200,000 ($300,000 with a spouse) or net worth above $1 million excluding your home. Accreditation is never part of joining. It is certified only when a deal opens for you.

Where your money lives

Your capital stays in your own account until you choose a company. Silver & Salt Capital is not a fund: no one hands us money to manage, and nobody invests your money but you.

The relationship requirement

Investing with us begins with a relationship, which is why every membership starts with a conversation. We get to know you first, and deals are discussed privately with members we know.
